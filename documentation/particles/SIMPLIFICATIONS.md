# Particle lab engine — simplifications

Every approximation of the model, with why it is there and what it changes. The "About this model" panel shows the
same list to pupils (Polish first, two levels of wording) — that models simplify reality is itself a lesson. When a
milestone adds or removes an approximation, this file changes with the code. Architecture:
[ARCHITECTURE.md](ARCHITECTURE.md). Rows marked (M1b), (M1c) describe what those milestones will bring; the others
are in the code since M1a.

| # | Simplification | Why | What it changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A 2D slab.** The box is a slab of depth D (1 cm); nothing moves in the third direction and the front and back faces are not modelled. | 3D at this resolution is far beyond a PC in real time. | Flows are planar; a "bubble" is a disc of depth D, a beaker a cross-section, surface tension curves only in the plane. Masses and energies are real for the slab. |
| 2 | **Matter in particles of 2–3 mm** (2 mm normally, 3 mm on a weaker computer). Each particle carries a volume s²·D of one material or mixture. | The particle count decides the speed. | Anything smaller than a few particles (thin films, tiny bubbles, a single droplet of dew) is not resolved; surfaces look grainy. Walls are at least two particles thick, so at 3 mm a beaker holds about 10 % less water than at 2 mm. |
| 3 | **Gas densities are compressed for motion.** Real water is about 1 600 times denser than steam; the solver moves gases with densities mapped (log scale, order kept) into 20–80 kg/m³ (air 61, steam 52). | No real-time particle solver is stable with a 1 600:1 ratio. | Buoyancy of gas in liquid is right in direction and order, but hot air rises more slowly than in reality (density differences between gases shrink), and a column of air weighs about 50 times too much in the solver (a few Pa over the band, see #17). Heat, phase change and all displayed densities use real values. |
| 4 | **Low-Mach gas.** Gas density follows temperature and composition at ambient pressure; local pressure differences only drive flow. | The standard model for slow, hot gas flows; it keeps the step large. | No sound, no pressure waves, no compression of gas by a piston; explosions are out of reach (a later milestone needs a compressible model). |
| 5 | **Vapour expands at most K_max ≈ 16 times** (M1c). A boiling particle splits into at most 16 vapour particles instead of growing 1 600 times in volume. | Splitting keeps the resolution; 1 600 particles per evaporated particle would flood the box. | A vapour particle holds about 100 times more water than real steam of its volume: bubbles are smaller than real for the same evaporated mass. Mass and energy budgets stay exact. |
| 6 | **Incompressibility is approximate.** The density constraint gets one iteration per substep, eight substeps per step at 2 mm (six at 3 mm), with half corrections (ω = 0.5). | Real-time budget; full corrections overshoot. | Water compresses by 1–2 % at the bottom of a 5 cm column, the surface sits about that much lower. The pressure shown comes from the solver, corrected for the discrete gradient and smoothed over 50 ms: 95–96 Pa per cm of water against 97.9 (hydrostatic test). |
| 7 | **Viscosity is numerical.** A smoothing of neighbour velocities (XSPH) in every substep keeps the fluid quiet: 0.03 between liquid particles, 0.08 wherever gas is involved, 0.03 at walls. The real viscosity of water would be negligible at this resolution. | The iterations leave a small unrest; light gas particles take most of it. | Water behaves more like a thin syrup than like water: small eddies die quickly. Gas flows are smoothed even more (rising steam, M1c, will be broad and calm). Water at rest still trembles by about 0.5 cm/s (rms). |
| 8 | **Surface tension and wetting are a cohesion model** (Akinci et al. 2013). The cohesion follows from the surface tension of water by a formula, divided by 16 so the Laplace pressure of a disc is right (±10 %); adhesion to glass, steel and ceramic follows from the contact angle (Young–Dupré), not measured. | Real surface tension acts on scales far below a particle. | Droplet pressure is right; droplet shapes and contact angles are approximate (a meniscus is about one particle). |
| 9 | **Nucleation is a superheat threshold** (M1c). Liquid starts to boil only above the saturation temperature plus ΔT_nuc — small at rough surface spots (boiling chips, metal), large in the bulk. | Bubble nuclei (micro-crevices, dissolved gas) are far below particle size. | Superheating and bumping appear, but the onset of boiling depends on the chosen ΔT values, not on the true microscopic surface. |
| 10 | **No dissolved gas in water.** Air does not dissolve in water. | Not needed for boiling. | The small bubbles of dissolved air that appear early when heating real water are missing. |
| 11 | **Condensate collects before it becomes a droplet** (M1c). Water condensing from a gas particle goes to an accumulator on a nearby liquid or solid particle; a droplet appears when a particle's worth has collected. | A droplet cannot be smaller than a particle. | Dew appears in steps; very thin fog or mist is not shown. |
| 12 | **Constant heat capacities per phase** (M1b), a simple conductivity of water against temperature, latent heat of vaporisation varying only by Kirchhoff's law, ideal gas mixtures, dry air = N₂ + O₂. | Enough for the effects shown; data are sourced in `species.json`. | Small errors (≈ 1 %) in heating rates and boiling temperatures away from 1 atm; no argon or CO₂ in air. |
| 13 | **Heat loss through a transfer coefficient** (M1b). Walls and open sides exchange heat with the room through one coefficient; no radiation. | Radiation and natural convection outside the box are not part of the scene. | Cooling rates are approximate; a hot plate does not glow. |
| 14 | **Apparatus is rigid.** Beakers, plates and walls are static particles: they conduct heat (M1b) but do not move, deform, crack or melt. | Solids that move and melt come in a later milestone. | A beaker cannot be knocked over; glass never breaks. |
| 15 | **The room outside an open side is still ambient air.** Beyond an open side the room is air without particles, like the air far from everything inside the box (#17); what leaves the box is removed — substances counted as escaped, air returned to the room. | The room is not simulated. | Steam leaving the box is counted, not followed; air coming in is always at ambient conditions. |
| 16 | **Time steps are fixed (1/240 s)**, divided into 6–8 substeps (more, up to 16, when something moves fast; beyond that speed is capped at about 6 m/s at 2 mm). Fast-forward runs more steps per frame, as far as the device allows. | Stability and determinism. | On a slow device time may run slower than real time; the lab says so and offers the all-cores tab or a lower quality, and the developer HUD shows the numbers. The results do not change, only how fast they come. |
| 17 | **Air only where it matters.** Air is made of particles only in a band about 12 mm wide (6 particles) around liquids, painted things, other gases, and air or apparatus warmer or colder than the room. Everywhere else the box holds ambient air without particles: still, at room temperature, pressure and composition. It holds the band's air like a wall of still air; air that moves between the band and the room is booked, so the budgets stay exact. | A box full of air particles costs 10–30 times more than the air that takes part (13 427 instead of 458 particles in the boiling scene). | There are no air currents far from everything (warm air pulls the band along, M1b). The band's edge is a line of cells and can look boxy; its outermost air trembles a little more. Pupils see air particles only near things — the side panel says that the rest of the box is air too. |

M1a note: the "keep apart" constraint of M0 is gone; the mechanics are the PBF solver of ARCHITECTURE.md §6.2.
