# 2D lab engine (v2) — simplifications

Every approximation of the model, with why it is there and what it changes. The "About this model" panel shows the
same list to students (Polish first, two levels of wording, `i18n.ts` → `SIMPLIFICATIONS_PL`) — that models simplify
reality is itself a lesson. When a milestone adds or removes an approximation, this file changes with the code.
Architecture: [ARCHITECTURE.md](ARCHITECTURE.md). Numbers: [MILESTONES.md](MILESTONES.md).

| # | Simplification | Why | What it changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A 2D slab.** The box is a slab of depth D (1 cm); nothing moves across it, and the front and back faces neither lose heat nor touch anything. | 3D at this resolution is beyond a school laptop in real time. | Flows are planar. Masses and energies are real for the slab. |
| 2 | **Fields on cells of 1–3 mm.** Temperature, pressure, gas velocity, composition and fog are one value per cell. | The cell count decides the speed. | Anything thinner than a cell (the thermal boundary layer at a hot wall, a thin film) is not resolved; the wall contact temperature (#9) stands in for the boundary layer. |
| 3 | **Separate liquid and gas solves.** The liquid sees the gas as a uniform pressure; the gas sees the liquid as a moving wall. | Water is about 830 times denser than air; one-way coupling of pressure is accurate for that ratio and far cheaper. | Air trapped in water, or air blown hard against a water surface, is not modelled (MultiFLIP is the upgrade path). |
| 4 | **Low-Mach gas.** Gas density follows temperature and composition at the region's pressure p0; local pressure differences only drive flow. | The standard model for slow, hot flows; it keeps the step large. | No sound, no pressure waves; explosions are out of reach (a later milestone needs a compressible solver). |
| 5 | **Constant-density liquid projection with a temperature-dependent body force.** | Exact hydrostatics with real densities, without the cost and instability of a variable-density projection. | Buoyancy of warm water is right; the liquid's pressure waves are not modelled (water is incompressible here; its pressure work V·Δp0 in a sealed box is booked). |
| 6 | **No surface tension on the resolved water surface.** | The capillary length of water (≈ 2.7 mm) is about one cell; resolving it needs a much smaller time step. Gravity therefore stays at 0.25 g or more. | Droplets and meniscuses on the cell scale take no shape from surface tension; zero gravity is not available in M1. Surface tension acts through the bubble models (pressure inside a bubble, nucleation, departure). |
| 7 | **No radiation.** Heat moves by conduction, convection and phase change only. | Radiation matters at a glowing plate, not in a boiling beaker. | A very hot plate loses less heat than in reality. It is drawn redder above 60 °C as a hint, not as computed glow. |
| 8 | **Heat loss through one coefficient** h_ext = 10 W/(m²·K) on the box walls, in series with half a cell. | The room outside is not simulated. | Cooling is approximate; the front and back of the slab lose nothing (#1). |
| 9 | **Wall contact temperature.** Where liquid touches a solid, nucleation and attached bubbles use T_f = (k_s T_s + k_l T_l)/(k_s + k_l). | Cells of 2 mm cannot resolve the thermal boundary layer; without this the wall overheats by tens of kelvin. | Superheat at the wall is approximate; nucleation onsets follow the cavity model, not a resolved boundary layer. |
| 10 | **Cavities as a sampled list per surface face.** At most eight per solid face, mouth radii from a truncated power law per material (steel calibrated to Lemmert & Chawla at 1 atm; glass at most 3 µm, boiling chips up to 150 µm). | Cavities are microscopic. | Where and when bubbles form is statistically right for the material, not for a particular real surface. Moving a solid redraws its cavities. |
| 11 | **Bubbles with correlations.** Growth (Plesset–Zwick, Ranz–Marshall), departure (Fritz), drag (Tomiyama, contaminated water); no lift, no bubble collisions except coalescence. | Bubbles are smaller than or comparable to a cell; their flow cannot be resolved. | Bubble sizes, growth and rise speeds follow the correlations within their accuracy (about 10–20 %); bubble shapes are not drawn deformed. |
| 12 | **Ideal gases**, dry air = N₂ + O₂, vapour enthalpy depending on temperature only; gas properties read from 1 K tables. | Accurate within a fraction of a per cent at these pressures. | The latent heat away from 100 °C follows Kirchhoff's law from the IF97 value at 100 °C (0.5 % low at 20 °C). |
| 13 | **Fog is a field, not droplets.** Condensed water in the gas is a mass per cell that moves with the gas, without falling. | Fog droplets are microscopic. | Fog does not settle or rain out; dew forms only on walls. |
| 14 | **The room beyond an open side is ambient air.** Inflow brings ambient air; what leaves is booked as escaped. | The room is not simulated. | Steam leaving the box is counted, not followed. |
| 15 | **Solids are rigid wholes** (M3a). A beaker, a plate, a block is a rigid body of exact rectangles (#33): it falls, floats, sinks, slides and tips, but does not bend or break; ice melts and grows (#40); pinned ones (beakers and plates unless the scene frees them) never move. The drag tool moves a body instantly by the exact offset (by whole cells when a painted solid is in the region), with the water and bubbles inside (the air stays). | Rigid bodies take the stiff limit of glass, stone and ice exactly at no cost (an elastic solid would need microsecond steps, SANDBOX.md §2). | A dropped beaker does not shatter; ice does not crack; a dragged beaker does not slosh. |
| 16 | **Free-slip walls for the liquid.** At the box walls the APIC stencils are completed by mirrored ghost nodes; solids push particles out along their distance field. | A no-slip boundary layer is far thinner than a cell; truncated stencils also made water climb the walls. | Water slides along walls without friction; near-wall flows are slightly too fast. |
| 17 | **No gas pockets in the water except bubbles.** A cell may turn into gas only if it was gas, touches gas or touches an open side. | A void inside the liquid would have no pressure law in this model (like a liquid region without a free surface, which the tools refuse). | No cavitation; air entrained by splashing closes up instead of forming pockets. |
| 18 | **Many small bubbles at a hot wall.** One wall cell holds the whole local wall superheat, so a cavity is ready again as soon as its cell is warm. | The thin superheated layer that really limits re-nucleation is not resolved (#2, #9). | In subcooled water thousands of tiny bubbles per second form and collapse at the bottom (energy is right; the count is too high). |
| 19 | **Bubbles inside the slab.** Spheres until they are as wide as the slab, then flattened, then discs of depth D; the Laplace pressure stays 2σ/r for discs. Bubbles below 20 µm move with the water. | The slab has a depth; a disc's real meniscus adds about 2σ/D (15 Pa at 1 cm). | Large bubbles are slightly too easy to compress (15 Pa of about 100 kPa); tiny bubbles do not slip. |
| 20 | **Bursts spread over the gas above.** A bursting bubble gives its content to as many gas cells above it as its volume needs; the gas volume correction is capped at ±½ cell per step, and the liquid makes room for bubbles (or closes in behind them) at most 200 cell volumes per second. | A large bubble bursting into one small cell would demand a velocity the transport cannot carry; a merge or collapse within one short step demanded liquid speeds ∝ 1/Δt. | A big burst is pushed out over a few steps (a short overpressure) instead of as an instant jet; a big merge or collapse is displaced over a few steps. Ordinary boiling never reaches the limit. |
| 21 | **Fog opacity is a drawing assumption.** White with opacity from Beer–Lambert for droplets of 1.5 µm radius seen through 7 cm (a beaker's width), not through the 1 cm slab. | Through 1 cm real steam fog would be almost invisible. | The fog mass is computed; how white it looks is an assumption (with 1 µm droplets it would look denser, with 5 µm thinner). |
| 22 | **Several liquids share one velocity field** (M2a). Each particle keeps its own liquid and density; the projection uses the mean density, and gravity acts on each particle's own density (#5 extended). | One pressure solve for all liquids, with exact hydrostatics in every layer. | Inertia uses the mean density (a Boussinesq-type approximation): right for density ratios of about 0.7–1.5 (oil/water 0.92, brine/water 1.2), not for mercury or molten metal (they need a variable-density projection). |
| 23 | **Viscosity in a simplified form, only where it matters.** Implicit ∇·(η∇u) per velocity component without the ∇·(η (∇u)ᵀ) part; the liquid sticks to walls and solids, the free surface carries no stress. Computed only when a liquid other than water is present; water stays inviscid at the cell scale. | Water's viscosity is negligible on 1–3 mm cells; oil is about 100 times more viscous at 20 °C and needs it. The full stress form costs more and differs only where viscosity varies sharply. | Viscous liquids flow and settle realistically; at an oil–water interface the shear transfer is approximate. Water costs nothing extra (no viscosity solve without another liquid). |
| 24 | **No surface tension between liquids; unresolved droplets drift.** A particle surrounded by another liquid (the minority in its cell) is a droplet of its own volume and moves relative to the flow at its terminal velocity (Schiller–Naumann drag with the Hadamard–Rybczynski factor). | Without the drift, a cell with two water and two oil particles has the mean density and no buoyancy: emulsions would never separate below the cell size. | Mixed liquids separate, the interface is as sharp as the particles allow. The droplet size is the particle size (2.7 mm at 2 mm cells): a real fine emulsion separates more slowly than here. Oil drops in water stay angular (no rounding by surface tension). |
| 25 | **Only water is volatile.** Oil does not evaporate, smoke or burn; outside the measured range (20–160 °C) its laws are extrapolated. A steam bubble in oil exchanges no mass (it neither grows nor condenses — it rises and bursts); vapour condenses only on water, not on an oil surface. | Vegetable oils have a negligible vapour pressure below their smoke point; boiling in oil and condensation into it would need nucleation and droplets at liquid–liquid interfaces (a later scenario). | Water under oil boils correctly and its steam crosses the oil. Water dropped into hot oil does not spit (no explosive boiling of droplets yet). Dew does not form on oil; fog forms in the gas instead. |
| 26 | **Oil's surface tension is constant.** 33.4 mN/m, the mean of the literature values at 20 °C (Melo-Espinosa et al. 2014). | The cited data give 20 °C only; the value matters only for bubbles crossing the oil. | Near 150 °C the real value is roughly 10 mN/m lower: bubbles in hot oil are slightly too round in the drag law. |
| 27 | **One salt, NaCl** (M2b). A water particle carries its dissolved salt; one water activity (Pitzer, parameters of PHREEQC, below 0 °C those of Spencer, Møller & Weare 1990) gives the vapour pressure, the boiling point and the freezing point; density and heat capacity follow Laliberté (2009). Brine conducts heat, flows (inviscid at the cell scale, like water) and wets the bubbles with water's conductivity, viscosity and surface tension. | One cited model for all colligative effects keeps them consistent with each other; the other properties of brine differ from water's by a few to tens of per cent where they hardly matter on 2 mm cells. | Boiling and freezing points agree with the data to hundredths of a kelvin (MILESTONES.md §M2b). Bubbles in strong brine rise and grow as in water (real brine is up to twice as viscous); other salts and mixtures of salts are not available. |
| 28 | **Salt diffuses at its molecular rate with one D; stirring sharpens it into striations.** Inside a cell each water particle moves toward the cell's salt per kg of water. In still water at the rate of the slowest diffusion mode a cell holds, τ = Δx²/(π² D) (4.8 min at 2 mm and 20 °C, 1.4 min at 80 °C). Where the water flows, faster: the strain λ of the flow stretches what is in the cell into striations that thin as e^(−λt), and the cell is mixed when the time so far equals the diffusion time across a striation, t = W(2λτ)/(2λ) (W the Lambert function; the lamella picture of Ranz 1979 and Villermaux 2019) — 2.4 s instead of 4.8 min at λ = 1/s; strain below 0.02/s (the noise of water at rest) does not count. Between neighbouring cells salt flows by Fick's law (explicit, molecular D). D = 1.611·10⁻⁹ m²/s at 25 °C (the Nernst–Hartley mean of the ions' limiting diffusivities, CRC), other temperatures by Stokes–Einstein (D ∝ T/μ_w). Salt moves, its water stays with the particle. | Particles carry salt without numerical diffusion, so the physical diffusion has to be added; inside a cell it is what matters. Stirring creates gradients far finer than a cell (salt diffuses 600 times slower than momentum), so in moving water mixing is set by the stretching, not by the cell size. The fall of D with concentration (about 8 % at 1 mol/kg) and the counter-flow of water are small next to that. | Brine and fresh water stirred together even out in seconds where the water moves and in minutes where it rests, as in reality; a layer of brine under still water blurs by millimetres per hour. The striations themselves are not drawn: a cell is either mixed or not, within its mixing time. Between cells a wave one centimetre long decays 3 % slower than in the continuum at 2 mm (MILESTONES.md, tests 29, 30 and 35). |
| 29 | **Crystals go straight to the crust.** Where boiling or evaporation make a particle supersaturated (above the solubility of Pitzer, Peiper & Busey 1984), the excess crystallises at once and sits as a crust on the nearest solid below (a solid next to the particle, else the first one under it; on the bare box floor a store without heat exchange). The heat of crystallisation stays in the liquid. The crust does not dissolve again. | Salt grains, their settling and dissolution need the granular milestone (M3c); without supersaturation the brine never exceeds its solubility. | A boiled-dry beaker keeps all its salt as a white crust on the bottom (exactly). No crystals float in the brine, no crust creeps up the walls, and water poured onto a crust leaves it in place until M3c. Below 0 °C the solubility is held at its 0 °C value (hydrohalite, NaCl·2H₂O, is not modelled). |
| 30 | **Droplets below the cell size.** Particles in a cell that is not liquid (a splash, the last remains of an evaporating layer) boil off when they are above their boiling point (vapour into their own cell) and fly ballistically when they are two cells from any liquid; a droplet that boils down to nothing leaves its salt as crust. They do not evaporate below the boiling point. | A cell is liquid only when it is at least half full; before M2b such particles hovered where they were and a hot plate heated them far past boiling. | A brine layer boiled on a hot plate dries completely; a droplet resting on a cold surface stays (real one would dry in minutes). Splashes thrown out of the box are booked as escaped. |
| 31 | **Ice melts and freezes at an interface at T_f of the liquid in front** (M3b). The freezing point comes from the water activity (#27; for brine stronger than the eutectic, 23.2 %, the eutectic temperature −21.2 °C, where it solidifies completely after hydrohalite has formed). Only ice bodies melt; a "solid" (painted) of ice is a wall that stays ice. New ice appears only on existing ice: no supercooling limit, no nucleation of ice in the bulk, no frost (vapour condenses on cold ice as a liquid film). | Melting and freezing need a surface to happen on; nucleating ice crystals in water is a separate process. | Water below 0 °C away from ice stays liquid (supercooled) however cold. A cold ice cube in humid air collects a water film, not frost. The vapour pressure below 0 °C uses p_sat at 0 °C (IF97 region 4 starts at 273.15 K). |
| 32 | **Particles of one cell share their heat at the rate of conduction across a cell.** The grid conducts heat between cells and gives the particles of a cell the same change; on top of that each particle moves toward its cell's mean temperature with τ = Δx²/(π² α), α = k/(ρ c_p) (water at 20 °C: 2.8 s at 2 mm, 0.7 s at 1 mm) — the model is IEM, interaction by exchange with the mean. The cell's energy stays exact. In liquid cells and at the surface; a droplet alone in the gas shares nothing. Unlike salt (#28), heat keeps this rate where the water flows. | Particles carry temperature without numerical diffusion: before this, a hot and a cold particle that the flow brought into one cell kept their temperatures for ever. Within 2.8 s a cell is even anyway; sharing faster carries the heat of a hot wall into the next cells through the particles' overlapping heat weights (the water in front of boiling chips stayed too cold and the glass boiled first — test 12). | Temperature differences below the cell size even out in seconds, as by conduction; finer structures share the slowest rate. The 2 mm grid still cannot resolve thinner boundary layers (#2, #9). |
| 33 | **Bodies are exact rectangles; the grid sees covered fractions** (since 2026-10-23; voxels before). Each grid cell and face knows the share of it the bodies cover; both projections use the open fractions of the faces and the bodies' velocity on the covered parts (cut cells, Batty, Bertails & Bridson 2007). A cell covered to 99 %, cut in two, or covered to half next to another solid (a box side, a painted wall, another body) is closed. | The water a body displaces changes continuously with its position (with whole cells, a floating block bobbed by rows); half-covered cells next to another solid are an unresolved gap. | A floating body rests within 0.06 mm (test 36). A gap of less than half a cell between a body and another solid is closed (water does not flow through it); the thin fluid part of a mixed cell can hold no particle for a while. A wall thinner than two cells lying across two cells is two half-open cells with a closed face between them: particles, evaporation and boiling respect the face, heat crosses it through the two cells' mixed conductivities (as if the glass were spread over both). Painted walls stay cells (staircases when tilted). |
| 34 | **Bodies and fluid are coupled step by step, the added mass measured.** The projections see the body's velocity of this step; their pressure moves the body for the next. The fluid's push-back to a change of velocity (the added mass) is measured each step with three more pressure solves and used to keep the coupling stable. | One pressure solve with the body's velocities as unknowns (Batty, Bertails & Bridson 2007) is the monolithic alternative; the measured added mass gives the same stability without a new solver. | A body reacts to the fluid one step late (8 ms at most); light bodies are stable. A body that has just woken uses an estimate for one step. |
| 35 | **The free surface is placed between cells by the volume fraction.** The gas pressure acts where φ crosses ½ between a liquid cell and its gas neighbour, at least a tenth of the distance from the liquid cell's centre. | Sub-cell accuracy of the absolute pressure decides how deep a body floats. | Where the particles of the surface jiggle the surface pressure jiggles with them: water at rest keeps currents of about 0.1 mm/s (0.003 mm/s with the surface at the gas cell's centre, as before M3a). |
| 36 | **The wake of a falling body is born from numerical asymmetries.** At cell scale the water has no viscosity; vortex shedding behind a body starts when its wake grows asymmetric. | Viscous boundary layers are far below the cell size. | A 2 cm square sinks 8–10 % faster than the drag law of a square cylinder (C_D = 2.1) for its first seven side lengths; then its wake turns it over and it tumbles and glides sideways — a chaotic path that differs between 1 and 2 mm (test 37). |
| 37 | **Contacts: one friction coefficient, restitution as a model parameter.** Coulomb friction √(μ_a μ_b) with one coefficient for sticking and sliding (sliding values from tables: glass 0.4, steel 0.42, oak 0.48, ice 0.02, rock 0.6), restitution max(e_a, e_b) between 0.1 and 0.3; contacts allow 0.1 cell of overlap. Box walls: μ 0.5, e 0.1. | The physics lab's contact solver (stable stacks, warm starts); static friction above the sliding value would need a second coefficient and state. | Things slide a little earlier than with real static friction; a beaker landing from a tilt slides a few millimetres. Wet surfaces and lubrication are not modelled. |
| 38 | **Sealed gas feels a moving body one step late.** A body displacing the gas of a sealed region compresses it at the next step (p0 from the state); the jump of −p0·V is booked as the body's work, the gas is not heated by the compression. The pressure on a body uses the p0 the liquid saw at the start of the step (a floating body in a sealed box used to be driven by the change of p0 within the step). | Sealed boxes with moving bodies are rare; the gas pressure is not part of the liquid projection. | A floating body in a small sealed box sloshes like in an open one; the energy budget with a body moving in a sealed region drifts by about 10⁻⁸ J per second (exact in open boxes). |
| 39 | **Bodies sleep.** A free body that has touched something and moved less than 2 mm/s for half a second stops being computed; it wakes when a moving body touches it, a tool changes the scene, the fluid lifts it with more than its weight, or it melts or grows. Free bodies settle for a moment after loading. | Costs scale with presence (SANDBOX.md §1): a beaker resting on a plate costs nothing. | A sleeping body on a slowly melting support wakes only when the support moves faster than 1 cm/s against it: it may hover a fraction of a millimetre above the shrinking ice for a moment. |
| 40 | **A melting or growing side moves as a whole** (M3b, the project's decision: a melting ice cube stays a rectangle). The heat of every internal cell above the interface temperature goes at once to a side of its rectangle — an edge cell to its sides with something warmer in front (water, air, a hot plate), shared by k (T − T_f), else and from inside to the nearest open side; a side's heat moves the side uniformly; sides of several rectangles on one line move together. Meltwater joins the particles in front of the side (or becomes a particle there once it has a quarter of a particle's mass); water colder than T_f in the two cells in front freezes onto the side, at most half of a particle's water per step. | Keeps the shape a few rectangles (the user's choice) with energy, water and salt exact; the heat inside the ice reaches its surface without the conduction delay. | Ice melts faster at its corners in reality and rounds; here a side recedes evenly and corners stay square. The interface lives in the cells: first order in Δx — the Stefan fronts are within 6 % (melting) and 7 % (freezing) of Neumann at 2 mm, 4 % and 3 % at 1 mm, the error coming from the first seconds when the thermal layer is thinner than a cell (MILESTONES.md §M3b). |
| 41 | **The accelerated view sees bodies as whole cells.** On the GPU a pinned body is the staircase of the cells it covers by more than half (or that the CPU closed; a thin wall on the boundary of two cells keeps one of them), with the body's temperature and the whole cell's heat capacity; free bodies and ice run on the CPU only. | The GPU kernels have no cut cells; the presets' beaker and plate lie exactly on the 1 mm cells, so there the staircase is the body. | A body placed between cells holds up to half a cell more or less glass on the GPU (the heating rate of test 3 within 3 %); it is drawn as cells there. |

## Performance-related choices

These do not change the physics model, but they decide what a student's computer can show (MILESTONES.md):

- dt = min(1/120 s, 0.9 Δx/u_max): real time needs two steps per 60 Hz frame. A slower computer runs the same
  simulation in slow motion; the shell says so and offers a coarser cell.
- Pressure solves stop at a relative residual of 1e-5 (the prompt's real-time tolerance); the bookkeeping of mass and
  energy is exact regardless of solver tolerances.
- The accelerated view (WebGPU, ARCHITECTURE.md §15) computes in 32-bit floats: per-cell masses and energies are f32,
  the totals f64 on the CPU; its energy budget closes to 1e-5–4e-4 of the energy turned over (the CPU to 1e-9; the
  sealed box the most). Bubbles that share a cell draw on its heat budget at the same time, and merging does not follow
  the bubbles' order — the statistics of nucleation, merging and collapse differ by a few per cent from the CPU, and
  runs are not repeatable. Heat and vapour diffusion use multigrid there instead of Jacobi preconditioning (same
  tolerance).
- Baked clips (B, ARCHITECTURE.md §16) store what is visible at display precision: temperature in 0.5 K steps,
  pressure, speed and the arrow velocities on fixed quadratic 8-bit scales, humidity in 64 steps; the live view draws
  from the same layers, so playback shows exactly what the live view showed. The layers that only the gas overlays
  show (temperature, pressure, speed, humidity, vapour, bubbles in the phase map, arrows) are stored in every third
  frame and held in between; the frame without overlay and the liquid are stored in every frame. Clips hold no single
  particles (the particle view works live). None of this touches the computation: a clip continued live is bit for bit
  the run it was baked from.
